A path to learning tarot

Work through these in order. Each stage builds on the last. Use Flashcards and Quiz to drill what you have covered, and Daily Card every morning to practise reading in context.

Stage 0 — How the deck is built

  • 78 cards = 22 Major Arcana + 56 Minor Arcana.
  • Major Arcana (0–21): life's big themes and turning points — "The Fool's Journey".
  • Minor Arcana: four suits of 14 (Ace–10 + Page, Knight, Queen, King) — everyday life.
  • Suits & elements: Wands=Fire (drive, creativity), Cups=Water (emotion, relationships), Swords=Air (thought, conflict), Pentacles=Earth (money, body, work).

Stage 1 — The four suits + numbers 1–10

Learn each suit's "mood", then the number pattern that runs through all suits.

Drill: Flashcards → mode "Image → meaning", filter to one suit at a time.

Stage 2 — The 16 court cards

  • Page = student / message / new energy of the suit.
  • Knight = action / pursuit / the suit taken to an extreme.
  • Queen = mature inward mastery / nurturing the suit's power.
  • King = mature outward mastery / authority in the suit's domain.

Trick: read a court card as "a person (or part of you) who is [suit mood] in a [rank] way."

Stage 3 — The Major Arcana as a story

The Fool (0) walks through 21 encounters. Learn them in three arcs of 7:

  • 1–7 (the outer world): Magician → Chariot. Building an identity and will.
  • 8–14 (the inner world): Strength → Temperance. Meeting the self, loss, and balance.
  • 15–21 (the higher world): Devil → World. Breaking illusion, rebirth, wholeness.

Drill: Quiz → "Major Arcana order".

Stage 4 — Reversals

A reversed card usually means the energy is blocked, internal, delayed, or overdone — not simply "the opposite". Learn uprights solidly first; add reversed keywords once uprights are automatic.

Stage 5 — Reading spreads

Move from single cards to combinations. Start with the 3-card spread, then Celtic Cross. See the Spreads tab for layouts and a practice mode.

How to practise a reading
  1. Shuffle, ask a clear open question ("What do I need to know about…?").
  2. Lay the spread. Note your gut reaction to each image before recalling keywords.
  3. Read each position, then the cards as a sentence together.
  4. Write it down. Check meanings after. Revisit in a week.

A quick read on any pip = suit's element + number theme. E.g. 5 of Cups = a loss (5) in the emotional realm (Cups).

Reading one card three ways

The same card answers differently depending on the question. In the Browse tab, every card now has an "In a reading" panel with three lenses:

  • Love & relationships — how the card's energy plays out between people.
  • Work & money — how it plays out in your projects, career and resources.
  • Advice / card for today — what to actually do with it.

Practice: draw a Daily Card, decide which lens fits your current situation, then read it through that one.

Symbolism — why the pictures matter

Waite said the tarot "speaks no other language" than symbolism. Pamela Colman Smith's innovation in 1909 was to illustrate every card — including the number cards — with a full scene, so the deck can be read from the images alone.

Each card entry in Browse lists its notable symbols and what they point to. Learn to notice recurring motifs: a white rose (pure intent), the infinity sign (unlimited energy), pillars (a threshold), falling Yods (grace), mountains (challenges or achievement), a figure with one foot in water (conscious and unconscious in balance).

A little history

  • ~1430s, northern Italy — tarot appears as a trick-taking card game with an extra set of trump cards. No divinatory meaning for centuries.
  • 1781, France — Court de Gébelin popularises the idea that the cards hold ancient esoteric wisdom; occult interest grows through the 19th century.
  • late 1800s — the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn systematises correspondences between tarot, astrology, the elements and the Kabbalah.
  • 1909 — Arthur E. Waite and artist Pamela Colman Smith (both former Golden Dawn members) publish the deck through William Rider & Son, London. Its fully-illustrated pips made it the template for most modern decks.

Daily routine (15 min)

  • Flashcards: clear today's due pile.
  • Daily Card: draw, journal one line, guess meaning, reveal.
  • Once a week: one full 3-card practice reading.

Progress uses a 5-box Leitner system saved in this browser. "Again" sends a card to box 1; "Good"/"Easy" promote it. Higher boxes reappear less often.
